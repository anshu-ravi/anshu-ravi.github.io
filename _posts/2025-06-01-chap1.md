# From Text Prediction to Conversation: How AI Models Actually Work

*This post is part of my journey through "AI Engineering" by Chip Hyuen, where I'm converting my study notes into accessible explanations. As I work through each chapter, I'm attempting to distill complex technical concepts into something more digestible—both for my own understanding and for anyone else navigating this rapidly evolving field.*


You've probably had that moment—asking ChatGPT a question and getting back a response so natural that you forget you're talking to a machine. But here's something that might surprise you: underneath all that conversational polish, these AI models are essentially very sophisticated autocomplete systems. They're not thinking about your question in the way you might imagine. Instead, they're running incredibly complex statistical calculations to predict what word should come next.

This distinction matters more than you might think. Understanding how AI models actually work—versus how they appear to work—is crucial for anyone building applications with them. It's the difference between using a tool effectively and being constantly surprised by its limitations.

## The Token Economy: How Machines Read Text

Before we dive into how AI models generate responses, let's start with something fundamental: how they "read" text in the first place. Unlike humans who process words as complete units, AI models work with something called tokens.

Think of tokens as the basic building blocks of language that machines can understand. A token might be a whole word like "cat" or "running," but it could also be part of a word like "cook" and "ing" from "cooking." This isn't arbitrary—there's actually clever reasoning behind this approach.

Consider a word like "chatgpting" (yes, that's become a verb). A human might struggle with this made-up word, but an AI model can break it down into "chatgpt" and "ing." Suddenly, the structure makes sense—it's following the same pattern as "walking" or "running." This tokenization process helps models handle new words and understand linguistic patterns they've never seen before.

The vocabulary size—the total number of tokens a model can work with—varies dramatically between models. GPT-4 has over 100,000 tokens in its vocabulary, while some smaller models work with around 32,000. This is like comparing someone who knows 100,000 words versus someone who knows 32,000—the larger vocabulary generally enables more nuanced understanding and expression.

## Two Fundamentally Different Approaches to Language

Not all language models are created equal. There are two main architectural approaches, and they're optimized for completely different tasks.

**Masked Language Models** are like very sophisticated fill-in-the-blank systems. Show them a sentence like "The cat sat on the ___" and they'll predict "mat" by considering the entire context—both what comes before and after the blank. BERT, one of the most famous models in this category, excels at understanding context and is widely used for tasks like sentiment analysis and text classification.

**Autoregressive Language Models** work differently. They can only look backward, predicting the next word based solely on what came before. This might seem limiting, but it's actually perfect for generating text. When you ask ChatGPT to write a story, it starts with your prompt and generates one word at a time, using only the words it's already written to decide what comes next.

Here's the key insight: that constraint—only being able to look backward—is what makes these models so good at generating coherent, flowing text. They're forced to maintain consistency with everything they've already said.

## The Journey from Prediction Engine to Conversational Partner

This brings us to a crucial point that many people miss: the model that predicts the next word is not the same as the chatbot you interact with. That transformation happens through something called post-training.

During pre-training, a model learns to be a next-word prediction engine by reading vast amounts of internet text. It learns patterns, facts, and relationships, but it doesn't learn to be helpful or to have conversations. If you asked a pre-trained model "What's the weather like?", it might respond with "What's the weather like in your city?" or "What's the weather like today versus yesterday?" because it's simply continuing the pattern it sees most often online.

Post-training is where the magic happens. This is where models learn to respond to questions, follow instructions, and engage in dialogue. It's a bit like teaching someone who's memorized every book in the library how to actually help library visitors find what they need.

## Beyond Text: The Expanding Universe of AI Understanding

The principles that make language models work have proven surprisingly adaptable to other types of data. OpenAI's CLIP model, for instance, learned to understand images by studying millions of image-caption pairs found naturally on the internet. Instead of requiring humans to manually label each image, CLIP learned from the captions that already existed alongside images online.

This approach has expanded even further. Recent models like ImageBind create shared understanding across six different types of data: text, images, audio, video, depth information, and even thermal data. The implications are profound—these models can understand that a picture of a dog, the word "dog," and the sound of barking are all related concepts.

## The Scaling Revolution

One of the most remarkable aspects of modern AI development is how consistently performance improves with scale. Bigger models, trained on more data with more compute, reliably perform better. This isn't just true for language—it's a pattern that holds across different types of AI systems.

Consider the progression of just one model family: Llama 1 was trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, Llama 2 on 2 trillion tokens, and Llama 3 on 15 trillion tokens. Each version significantly outperformed its predecessor, and the improvements weren't just incremental—they often unlocked entirely new capabilities.

This scaling phenomenon has given rise to what researchers call "scaling laws"—mathematical relationships that help predict how much better a model will perform if you double its size or training data. The Chinchilla scaling law, developed by DeepMind, suggests that for optimal performance, you need about 20 tokens of training data for every parameter in your model. So a 3-billion parameter model should ideally be trained on about 60 billion tokens.

## The Efficiency Frontier: When Bigger Isn't Always Better

But here's where things get interesting: the relationship between model size and performance isn't as straightforward as it first appears. Some models use techniques like sparsity—where many of the parameters are zeros—to achieve better performance with less actual computation.

Mixture of Experts (MoE) models exemplify this approach. Take Mixtral 8x7B, which has 8 different "expert" networks, each with 7 billion parameters. In theory, that's 56 billion parameters total, but for any given input, only 2 experts are active. This means the model has the capacity of a 56-billion parameter model but only uses 12.9 billion parameters for each prediction.

This is revolutionary because it means you can have a very large, capable model that doesn't require enormous computational resources to run. It's like having a library with eight different specialists, but you only need to consult two of them for any given question.

## Making Sense of Model Capabilities

When evaluating AI models, three numbers matter most: the number of parameters (learning capacity), the number of training tokens (how much the model has learned), and the number of FLOPs (computational cost). These create a triangle of trade-offs that define what's possible with current technology.

Understanding these relationships helps explain why AI development has become so expensive and why only a few organizations can train the largest models. It also helps predict where the technology is heading and what kinds of improvements we might see in the future.

## The Road Ahead

What strikes me most about this field is how much the fundamental insights from language modeling have influenced other areas of AI. The idea that you can train a model to predict the next token in a sequence has proven to be remarkably general—it works for text, code, images, and even scientific data.

This suggests we're still in the early stages of understanding what's possible. As models get better at predicting sequences, they're becoming more capable of reasoning, creating, and problem-solving. The line between "predicting the next word" and "thinking" continues to blur.

The key insight for anyone working with AI is this: these models are fundamentally pattern recognition systems trained on human-generated data. They're incredibly sophisticated, but they're not magic. Understanding how they work—from tokens to transformers to post-training—gives you the foundation to use them effectively and build applications that truly serve human needs.

The future of AI isn't just about bigger models or more data. It's about understanding these systems well enough to align them with human values and deploy them in ways that amplify human capabilities rather than replace them. And that journey starts with understanding how these remarkable prediction engines learned to talk.
